# Zelda's Editorial Methodology

**A structured process for discovering, developing, and auditing the thematic core of _Boss at Work | Intern at Home: Port Yourself_.**

Load this as a project file in Claude Projects, or reference on demand when working through specific exercises. The companion `SYSTEM_PROMPT.md` implements this methodology; this document contains the full exercise instructions and editorial theory.

---

## Overview

Most nonfiction manuscripts fail not because the writing is bad, but because the book doesn't know what it's about. The author has ten interesting things to say but no single argument that makes all ten cohere. The result is a manuscript that drifts — each chapter competent in isolation, collectively forgettable.

This methodology is a six-phase editorial process that forces thematic clarity before, during, and after drafting. It draws on frameworks from Shawn Coyne (Story Grid), Steven Pressfield, William Zinsser, and working developmental editors like Megan Close Zavala and Andrea Stein.

The phases are sequential but not strictly linear — you'll often loop back from Phase 3 to Phase 1 as title work reveals theme weakness.

---

## Phase 1: Theme Discovery

**Goal:** Arrive at one sentence that captures the book's controlling idea.

**The problem this solves:** Authors usually start with a topic ("personal sovereignty"), not a thesis. A topic is a territory; a thesis is an argument about that territory. You can't build a book on a topic — you need a claim that can be proved, challenged, and defended.

> **For this book (resolved):** Controlling idea established and stress-tested through all four Phase 2 tests. See BOOK_CONTEXT.md Phase 1 and Phase 2 Findings.

### Exercise 1A: The "They Say / But I Say" Generator

Write **ten versions** of this sentence:

> "They say **\_**, but my experience tells me **\_**."

The left side captures the conventional wisdom your book challenges. The right side captures your counterintuitive claim. The version that creates the most cognitive dissonance — the one where readers would say "wait, really?" — is closest to your controlling idea.

**Why ten?** The first three will be safe. Versions 4–7 get more specific. Versions 8–10 often reveal the real argument you've been circling around.

**Source:** Marie Ponsot and Rosemary Deen's seed sentence technique, adapted for nonfiction by Bethany Saltman via Jane Friedman.

### Exercise 1B: Pressfield's Five Questions

Answer each in one sentence:

1. **What is the concept?** (The unique framing device — how this book approaches the topic differently from anything else)
2. **What is this about?** (The theme in its purest form)
3. **What is the beginning?** (Where the reader starts — their current worldview)
4. **What is the end?** (Where the reader arrives — their transformed worldview)
5. **Who is the villain?** (The antagonistic force your book fights against)

**Source:** Steven Pressfield, _Nobody Wants to Read Your Sh\*t_.

### Exercise 1C: The Coyne Controlling Idea

Write your book's argument as a single sentence that includes:

1. **A value at stake** (what's being gained or lost)
2. **A value change** (positive or negative shift)
3. **A cause** (why the shift happens)

Format: "[Value] is [gained/lost] when [cause]."

The sentence must be specific enough that someone could disagree with it. If no one would argue, it's too vague.

**Source:** Shawn Coyne, Story Grid methodology.

### Exercise 1D: The Zinsser Reduction

Complete this sentence: "After reading my book, the reader will carry away one thought they didn't have before, which is: **\_**."

If you can't reduce to one thought, you have multiple books competing in a single manuscript.

**Source:** William Zinsser, _On Writing Well_.

### Phase 1 Exit Criteria

You have a single sentence — your controlling idea — that:

- Fits on a Post-it note
- Contains a claim someone could disagree with
- Names a value change and its cause
- Creates cognitive dissonance when stated to your target reader

## Phase 2: Theme Stress Test

**Goal:** Pressure-test the controlling idea against reality before building a book on it.

### Exercise 2A: The Back-Cover Test

Write the two-paragraph description that would appear on the back of the finished book. Constraints:

- Paragraph 1: Name the problem your reader faces (in their language, not yours)
- Paragraph 2: State the promise of the book — what changes if they read it

If you spiral into a list of side topics, the controlling idea is too broad.

### Exercise 2B: The Antagonist Interrogation

Your book needs a villain — not a person, but a force. Answer:

1. What specific worldview does your book fight against?
2. What does your reader currently believe that your book will challenge?
3. What is the most sophisticated version of the argument against your thesis?
4. How does your book answer that argument?

If you can't articulate the strongest counterargument, your thesis isn't sharp enough.

### Exercise 2C: The Transformation Map

Define precisely:

| Dimension    | Before reading | After reading |
| ------------ | -------------- | ------------- |
| **Believes** | **\_**         | **\_**        |
| **Feels**    | **\_**         | **\_**        |
| **Does**     | **\_**         | **\_**        |

The reader should undergo all three shifts. If the book only changes beliefs but not behavior, it's an essay, not a self-help book.

### Exercise 2D: The Dinner Party Test

Imagine explaining your book's argument to a sharp, skeptical friend at a dinner party. You get **two sentences** — one to state the problem, one to state your answer. What do you say?

If their likely response is "well, obviously" — the idea is too safe.
If their likely response is "that's interesting, but..." — you're in the right zone.
If their likely response is "I don't follow" — the idea needs simplification.

### Phase 2 Exit Criteria

Your controlling idea survives all four stress tests. You can articulate the before/after transformation, the antagonist force, and the two-sentence dinner party pitch without hedging.

## Phase 3: Title & Subtitle Development

**Goal:** Find a title that sells the transformation and a subtitle that explains the mechanism.

**Core principle:** Self-help titles should name the solution, not emphasize the problem. The title creates an emotional response; the subtitle explains what the book will do for the reader.

> **For this book (resolved):** Working title: "Boss at Work | Intern at Home: Port Yourself." Two alternates banked. See BOOK_CONTEXT.md Phase 3 Findings.

### Exercise 3A: Archetype Analysis

Evaluate your controlling idea against each of the five title archetypes:

1. **Identity Label** — A single word or phrase readers adopt as self-description
   - Examples: _Essentialism_, _Grit_, _Range_
   - Test: Would readers say "I'm a [your word]ist" or "I practice [your word]"?

2. **Evocative Modifier + Familiar Noun** — An unexpected adjective transforms an ordinary concept
   - Examples: _Atomic Habits_, _Deep Work_, _Subtle Art_
   - Test: Does the modifier create surprise when paired with the noun?

3. **Declarative Thesis** — The title IS the argument
   - Examples: _Start With Why_, _The Obstacle Is the Way_
   - Test: Could this sentence end a debate?

4. **Counterintuitive Reframe** — Challenges conventional wisdom in the title itself
   - Examples: _The 4-Hour Workweek_, _The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F\*ck_
   - Test: Does it make someone say "wait, what?"

5. **The Binary** — Names both sides of the lens the book provides
   - Examples: The deep/shallow binary in _Deep Work_, the essential/nonessential binary in _Essentialism_
   - Test: Can readers use the two sides to evaluate their own daily decisions?

### The Title-Subtitle Division of Labor

| Component    | Job           | Tone                              |
| ------------ | ------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **Title**    | Grab the gut  | Emotional, evocative, memorable   |
| **Subtitle** | Tell and sell | Clear, specific, benefit-oriented |

The subtitle formula: "How to [mechanism] so you can [outcome]" or "A [framework] for [transformation]."

### Exercise 3B: The 25-Title Sprint

Set a 15-minute timer. Write 25 title candidates, no filtering. Include bad ones. Then:

1. Circle the 5 that create the strongest emotional response
2. For each, write 3 subtitle options
3. Test each title+subtitle combo against the controlling idea: does it promise exactly what the book delivers?

### Exercise 3C: The Category Shelf Test

Search Amazon for books in your category. Read the top 20 titles. Ask:

- Which titles would make me pick up the book?
- What pattern do the successful ones share?
- Does my title stand out on this shelf, or does it blend in?

### Phase 3 Exit Criteria

You have 3–5 title+subtitle candidates. Each one: names the transformation (not the problem), is distinct from competitors on the shelf, and accurately promises what the book delivers.

## Phase 4: Structural Architecture

**Goal:** Design the book's macro structure so that every chapter advances the controlling idea from a new angle.

> **For this book (resolved):** Arc A modified was chosen. The book uses four parts organized by the promotion ladder (The Trap / The First Win / The Promotion / The Director's Life). Nine chapters, designed backwards from the end state. Four gaps eliminated as scaffolding. See BOOK_CONTEXT.md Phase 4 Findings for the definitive structural outline with thread assignments.

### The Three Structural Arcs

Choose the macro structure that best serves your controlling idea:

**Arc A: Analyze / Formalize / Mechanize** (Story Grid)

- Part 1: Name the phenomenon. Show the reader what's really happening.
- Part 2: Formalize it into principles, frameworks, or a model.
- Part 3: Equip the reader to implement it in their own life.
- Best for: Books that introduce a new lens

**Arc B: Problem / Principles / Practice**

- Part 1: The problem in full — why it matters, why existing solutions fail.
- Part 2: The principles that solve it (3–5 max).
- Part 3: How to apply the principles in specific domains.
- Best for: Books with a clear prescriptive framework

**Arc C: The Progressive Deepening**

- Each chapter explores the same idea at a deeper level, like concentric circles.
- Chapter 1 gives the surface version. Final chapter gives the deepest version.
- Best for: Books with a single powerful insight that has many layers.

### Exercise 4A: The Chapter Thesis Ladder

For each planned chapter, write:

1. **Chapter thesis** (one sentence — what this chapter argues)
2. **Connection to controlling idea** (how this chapter advances the book's central argument)
3. **Reader state change** (what the reader believes/feels/does differently after this chapter)
4. **Evidence types** (stories, data, frameworks, exercises)

If any chapter's thesis doesn't connect to the controlling idea, it doesn't belong.

### Exercise 4B: The Metaphor System

Your book needs a central metaphor that:

- Extends naturally across many chapters (not a one-off analogy)
- Deepens with each use (readers understand it more richly each time)
- Creates vocabulary the reader carries forward

Map the metaphor to each chapter: what dimension of the metaphor does this chapter explore?

### Exercise 4C: The Four Quadrants (per chapter)

For each chapter, plan all four movements:

1. **Propose something unexpected** — complicate the reader's current understanding
2. **Make order** — organize the complication into a framework or principle
3. **Demonstrate in action** — narrative examples, case studies, data
4. **Equip to integrate** — exercises, reflection prompts, action steps

### Phase 4 Exit Criteria

You have a chapter-by-chapter outline where: every chapter has a clear thesis connected to the controlling idea, the metaphor system has a specific role in each chapter, and the macro arc is clear.

## Phase 5: Chapter Analysis

**Goal:** Evaluate each chapter of the drafted manuscript for thematic alignment, structural integrity, and editorial quality.

### The Chapter Scorecard

For each submitted chapter, evaluate:

| Dimension                 | Question                                                     | Score (1–5) |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------- |
| **Theme alignment**       | Does this chapter advance the controlling idea?              |             |
| **Thesis clarity**        | Can I state this chapter's argument in one sentence?         |             |
| **Evidence quality**      | Are claims supported by stories, data, or frameworks?        |             |
| **Four quadrants**        | Does the chapter propose, order, demonstrate, and equip?     |             |
| **Metaphor integration**  | Does the central metaphor appear and deepen?                 |             |
| **Voice consistency**     | Does the tone match the book's overall voice?                |             |
| **Reader transformation** | Does the reader's understanding change from start to finish? |             |
| **Necessity**             | If removed, would the controlling idea be weakened?          |             |

### The Five Self-Help Failure Modes (Andrea Stein / Gotham Ghostwriters)

Flag chapters that fall into:

1. **Selling, not teaching** — The chapter promotes the author rather than serving the reader
2. **Telling, not showing** — Abstract claims without concrete examples
3. **Repeating, not advancing** — Restating the same point instead of building on it
4. **Dropping, not developing** — Introducing concepts then abandoning them
5. **Multi-agenda** — The chapter tries to do too many things at once

### Feedback Format

1. State the chapter's thesis as you understand it (one sentence)
2. Score each dimension with specific textual evidence
3. Identify which failure modes apply (if any)
4. Give 2–3 specific, actionable revision directives
5. Note any "kill your darlings" candidates — material that's well-written but off-theme

### Phase 5 Exit Criteria

Every chapter scores 3+ on all dimensions. Failure modes are identified and addressed. Chapters that can't be fixed are flagged for cutting or restructuring.

## Phase 5.5: Chapter Blueprint (Ghostwriter Handoff)

**Goal:** Translate editorial analysis into a section-by-section writing plan that the ghostwriter can execute. This is the bridge between editorial direction and prose production.

**When to produce:** After Phase 5 analysis is complete for a chapter and the author has approved the revision directives. One blueprint per chapter.

**Who uses it:** The ghostwriter agent (`ghostwriter/`), which drafts and revises prose in the author's voice.

### The Blueprint Template

For each chapter, produce:

```markdown
# Chapter Blueprint: Ch [N] — [Title]

**Chapter thesis:** [One sentence]
**Word target:** [Range, e.g., 4,000-5,000]
**Source material:** [New / revision of old Ch X / combination]
**Revision intensity:** [% new material]

---

## Section-by-Section Plan

### Section 1: [Section title]

- **Purpose:** [What this section does — teaches, narrates, exercises, transitions]
- **Content:** [What goes here — specific frameworks, scenes, arguments]
- **Voice register:** [First person narrative / second person teaching / mixed]
- **Word target:** [Approximate]
- **Carries forward from:** [Existing prose reference, or "new"]
- **Key beats:**
  - [Specific moment, line, or move this section must land]
  - [Thread assignment this section serves]

### Section 2: [Section title]

[Same structure]

[Continue for all sections]

---

## Thread Checklist

- [ ] [Thread A assignment — where it lands]
- [ ] [Thread B assignment — where it lands]
      [All assigned threads for this chapter]

## Kill List (Do Not Include)

- [Material explicitly cut by Zelda's analysis]

## Author Input Needed

- [Specific personal stories, real numbers, or details only the author can provide]
- [Scenes that require lived experience, not invention]

## Ghostwriter Notes

- [Any voice-specific guidance for this chapter beyond the standard profile]
- [Sections where comedy should land vs. sections that should be straight]
- [Research citations to preserve, compress, or footnote]
```

### Blueprint Principles

1. **Every section has one job.** If a section is doing two things (teaching a framework AND landing a narrative beat), split it or choose one.

2. **Sequence narrative before teaching.** The author's voice profile demands confession before instruction. Blueprint sections should generally flow: author's experience -> framework definition -> reader application. Not always — but this is the default rhythm.

3. **Flag where the author must contribute.** The ghostwriter can write in the author's voice, but it can't invent the author's life. Real stories, real numbers, real names — mark these clearly so the author knows where to fill in.

4. **Include the kill list.** The ghostwriter needs to know what NOT to include as much as what to include. Material cut by Phase 5 analysis should be listed explicitly so it doesn't creep back in.

5. **Note comedy placement.** Not every section should be funny. The blueprint should indicate where structural comedy serves the argument (usually narrative sections and transitions) versus where straight delivery is more effective (usually core framework definitions and exercises).

6. **Respect the word budget.** Chapter targets are 4,000-6,000 words. If the section word targets sum to more than the chapter target, the blueprint is overscoped. Cut before writing, not after.

### Phase 5.5 Exit Criteria

The blueprint:

- Covers every section of the chapter with specific content direction
- Accounts for all thread assignments from Phase 4/5
- Identifies all author-input-needed moments
- Stays within the chapter's word budget
- Has been reviewed by the author before the ghostwriter begins

## Phase 6: Reverse Outline Audit

**Goal:** Test the complete manuscript for thematic drift, structural integrity, and reader experience.

### Step 1: Build the Reverse Outline

For each chapter, document:

- **Chapter thesis** (one sentence)
- **Controlling idea connection** (how it serves the central argument)
- **Primary evidence type** (story, data, framework, exercise)
- **Metaphor usage** (present/absent, shallow/deep)
- **Reader state after** (what changed)

### Step 2: Theme Strand Mapping

Assign color codes to the major thematic threads:

- **Red:** Controlling idea (must appear in every chapter)
- **Blue:** Supporting theme A
- **Green:** Supporting theme B
- **Yellow:** Supporting theme C
- **Gray:** Off-theme material

Map each chapter's content to these colors. A healthy manuscript shows red in every chapter, with blue/green/yellow rotating. Gray should be minimal.

### Step 3: Drift Detection

Look for these patterns:

- **The disappearing thread:** A theme appears in chapters 1–3, vanishes, then reappears in chapter 8
- **The rogue chapter:** A chapter with no red (controlling idea) content
- **The echo chapter:** Two chapters making essentially the same argument
- **The premature peak:** The most powerful version of the argument appears too early
- **The missing bridge:** Two chapters that should build on each other but don't connect

### Step 4: The "If I Removed This" Test

For every chapter: "If I removed this chapter, would the controlling idea be weakened?" If no — the chapter needs to be reframed, merged, or cut.

### Step 5: Kill Your Darlings (Save Them)

Material that doesn't serve the controlling idea moves to a separate file. Use it for: blog posts, newsletter content, social media, a potential second book, or talks. Nothing is wasted — it's just not for this book.

### Phase 6 Exit Criteria

The reverse outline shows: red (controlling idea) in every chapter, no rogue or echo chapters, no disappearing threads, and every chapter passes the removal test.
