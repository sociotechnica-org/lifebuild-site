## Directive 1: Rewrite Beat 6 through the credit card story (PRIMARY)

**The problem:** The four micro-skills are currently defined in second person with generic descriptions:

> **Externalize.** You got the problem out of your head and into words. Named it. In your head, it was vague and oversized. On paper, it had edges.
>
> **Decide.** You chose a method. Eliminate, delegate, automate, or reconfigure. Not just reacting to whatever felt urgent -- making a strategic choice about how to handle a specific problem.
>
> **Act.** You did it. Or handed it off. Or set up the system. The loop moved.
>
> **Complete.** You confirmed it's done. The open item closed. The drain stopped. The cognitive resources that the unfinished thing had been quietly consuming got released back into the system.

This is the chapter's primary voice gap. Each definition reads as a depersonalized instruction. None of it is anchored in the author's experience. The ghostwriter voice rules are explicit: "Never sustain second-person teaching for more than 150 words without returning to first-person anchoring." This section runs well past that limit.

**What to write:** Rewrite each micro-skill definition so it names the specific moment in the credit card story where that skill happened. The reader experienced Beat 5 moments ago -- the micro-skills should feel like a retrospective on what the author just showed them, not a new lecture.

Specifically:

- **Externalize:** The author wrote "credit card -- kids as authorized users" on a list. In his head, it was a vague cloud of guilt about college readiness. On paper, it was a ten-minute form. Name that moment.
- **Decide:** The author chose a method. He could have delegated it to his wife or automated a reminder. He didn't. He sat down and did it himself. That was a decision, not a default. Name the choice.
- **Act:** The author dug through his email, found the Fidelity link, found the SSNs in his tax return, filled out the form. Specific steps. Not "you did it" -- the author did specific things in a specific order.
- **Complete:** The ugly confirmation screen. The tickle from an angel feather. The walking around smiling. That's what completion feels like -- the drain stopped and the author could feel the resources come back. Name the screen. Name the feeling.

**Voice requirement:** First person dominant. Each micro-skill definition should be 2-3 sentences, anchored in the credit card story. The bold term names the skill; the sentences after it show the author doing it. The reader will recognize their own version without being told "you did this too."

**What to preserve from the current Beat 6:**

- The opening: "When the tab is settled, notice what happened." -- keep this, but follow it with the first-person credit card anchor that's already there ("Here's what I noticed after the credit card").
- The "Now notice what didn't happen" paragraph (lines 146-148). This passage works. It's second person but it follows the micro-skills, so by this point the first-person anchoring has been established. Keep it exactly.
- "That's an architectural move, not a willpower move. Structure, not character." -- keep exactly.
- "That's the pattern for everything that follows." -- keep exactly.

**What to cut or revise:**

- The final paragraph of Beat 6 (lines 154): "These four moves -- externalize, decide, act, complete -- are the loop that scales. Each of the next four chapters takes one of those micro-skills and builds it into a permanent capability. Visibility is externalization at life scale. Support is decision-making with a team behind it. Capacity protection is acting on your own behalf daily. Systems is the rhythm that keeps the loop completing."

  This is meta-commentary about book structure. The ghostwriter voice rules explicitly prohibit this: "The reader should never see the blueprint. They should feel the progression without being told about it." Cut the sentence-by-sentence mapping of micro-skills to chapters. Replace with something that names the four capabilities without explaining which chapter teaches which. The reader needs to know that each micro-skill scales into something bigger. They don't need the table of contents.

**Target length:** Beat 6 should run approximately the same length as the current version (~220-250 words). The micro-skill definitions will be slightly longer (specific stories vs. abstract definitions), but cutting the blueprint-narration paragraph compensates.

## Directive 2: Add one sentence connecting tabs to AI-era capacity (MINOR)

**The problem:** Ch 3 currently reads as generic personal-organization advice. There is no connection between "tabs consume cognitive bandwidth" and the AI-era capacity argument established in Ch 1 (cognitive concussion, the ratchet) and Ch 2 (bright red, the death spiral). Without this, the chapter could live in any productivity book from any era.

**What to write:** One sentence in Beat 2 (The Tab), inserted after the paragraph ending "...still yours, still undone." (line 34) and before the paragraph beginning "Interns let tabs run" (line 36). The sentence should connect the tab's cognitive cost to the capacity argument: tabs drain bandwidth you need for the factory work from Ch 1, the bright-red judgment calls from Ch 2. This is the sentence that locks Ch 3 into the book's larger argument rather than letting it float.

**Voice requirement:** This should read as a brief aside, not a thesis restatement. One sentence, maybe two. Think of how Ch 2 drops "Boss-level capacity burning on intern-level tasks" -- a single line that connects the chapter's specific argument to the book's larger one. Same move here. Something in the register of: the bandwidth these tabs consume is the same bandwidth your job demands -- the bright-red judgment calls from Ch 2 that empty the tank every day.

**Do not:** Restate the controlling idea. Do not use the phrase "AI economy" or "complex adaptive system." The reader already has that vocabulary from Ch 1. A glancing reference to the capacity cost is sufficient.

## Directive 3: Adjust Beat 7's closing line (MINOR)

**The problem:** Ch 2 ends with: "Part I showed you the trap. Part II shows you it breaks." Ch 3's Beat 7 ends with: "Part I showed you the trap. Part II showed you it breaks. Part III builds the thing that keeps it broken." The first two clauses are nearly identical. The Ch 3 version extends the device deliberately (adding Part III), which reads as intentional escalation -- but the repetition of the first two clauses risks feeling formulaic rather than earned.

**What to do:** Revise the closing line so it ARRIVES at the same destination (Part III builds the permanent infrastructure) without repeating Ch 2's exact phrasing. The line should feel like it completes the arc -- the reader has moved from diagnosis (Part I) through first win (Part II) and is now ready to build (Part III). But it should not echo Ch 2's sentence structure word-for-word.

**Preserve:** The line should still reference Parts I, II, and III. The progression (trap -> break -> build) is correct. Just vary the phrasing so it doesn't read as a copy-paste extension of Ch 2's close.

**Target length:** One to two sentences. No longer than the current version.
