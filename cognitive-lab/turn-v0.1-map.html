A framework for working with AI

The Turn

Working sustainably alongside AI agents that don't sleep

Act I — The new pace of work

Knowledge work used to come in batches

Mornings were emails. Afternoons were meetings. Projects had milestones. Reports had deadlines.

The work had a shape — discrete, planned, paced. You knew when you were on, when you were off, when something shipped, and when you'd done a day's worth. The tools you used — calendars, project software, email, status pages — were all designed for this rhythm. They assume the work pauses. They assume you do too.

This is the world most knowledge-work tools were designed for.

Act I — The new pace of work

AI made it continuous

AI agents work twenty-four hours a day. They don't pause.

They draft overnight. They review code while you sleep. They write emails before you've finished your coffee. The work doesn't stop. It doesn't even slow down. Output that used to be scarce is now effectively infinite — and every minute you're awake, more of it lands in your queue waiting for human judgment.

Act I — The new pace of work

The most AI-fluent people are breaking first

Not the people resisting the tools — the people using them most. They're the ones with ten open windows, the ones losing whole next-days to a great session yesterday, the ones who can't tell exhaustion from focus. They're shipping more than ever and feeling worse than ever, and they can't quite say why.

If you've felt this, you're not failing the tools. The tools are missing something.

Act II — Why batch tools fail

Continuous flow needs continuous management

The interface broke before the work did.

Calendars assume meetings. Project software assumes tasks. Status pages assume sign-offs. They were built to coordinate batched work between humans on a shared schedule. None of them were built to manage a stream of agent output landing twenty-four hours a day, with you as the only human who can decide what matters.

When the work stops pausing, the tools don't just lag — they stop being able to describe what's happening. So you fill in the gap with attention, browser tabs, and willpower. None of those scale.

Act II — Why batch tools fail

Imagine an air traffic control center with no controllers

Every pilot navigating their own approach. No radar. No scheduling. Forty planes in the pattern, each making local decisions with local information.

At first it works. The skies are big, the pilots are good, traffic flows. Then something goes wrong, and you can't find the data fast enough to fix it. Then it goes wrong again, faster. By dinner, it's plane crashes every fifteen minutes — not because the pilots are bad, but because there's no coordination layer.

That's AI-augmented knowledge work today. The pilots are good. The agents are powerful. The interface that lets a human direct the whole picture isn't there yet.

Act II — Why batch tools fail

What this actually feels like

Five symptoms most AI-fluent people will recognize:

Bright-red sessions. Work that felt great while you were doing it, but you're paying for the next morning. Dopamine masked the cost during.
Pushing 85 → 100. Going from "could go home whole" to "shipped" costs you the next day's six good hours.
Tab-roulette. Switching between agent windows while waiting on responses. You never get deep, you just get scattered.
Catatonic mornings. Paying for the night before. The body sends the bill late.
Frozen calls. Decisions you'd normally make easily, suddenly you can't. Decision fatigue arrives without warning.

These aren't moral failings. They're predictable results of running batch-shaped tools against continuous-flow work.

Act III — Borrowed wisdom

Continuous flow isn't actually new

It's new for knowledge work. It's not new for the world.

Air traffic control. Naval watch standing. Manufacturing lines. Hospital ICUs. Combat aviation. Long-haul shipping. Power grids. These have been running twenty-four-hour operations alongside humans for decades — sometimes centuries.

They've each had to solve some version of the same question: how do humans operate sustainably in a system that doesn't stop?

Act III — Borrowed wisdom

The science already exists

Forty years of research, sitting in the literature, mostly applied to industries you've heard of but haven't worked in. We're going to borrow what works.

Industries with answers

  • Aviation — pre-flight checklists, duty-time limits, pre-committed turnback points
  • Naval — watch standing, formal handoffs, "taking the conn"
  • Manufacturing — bottleneck management, andon, takt time
  • Medicine — surgical checklists, structured handoff protocols
  • Sport — load management, daily readiness, periodization

Science with answers

  • Cognitive load research — what kinds of mental load there are, how to reduce them
  • Recovery psychology — what actually restores capacity (and what only feels like rest)
  • Decision fatigue research — why sequential decisions degrade
  • Compensatory effort theory — why "feeling fine" can be the warning sign
  • Human factors — what happens when humans operate alongside automation

The framework here is mostly translation, not invention. We don't have to start from scratch.

Act IV — The framework

Match activity to capacity

The team that rests strategically will outperform the team that pushes continuously.

The principle the rest of this builds on. Read your capacity. Honor the reading. Decide what to do based on what you've actually got — not what you wish you had, not what the calendar says you should.

Aviation precedent. Pilots run a checklist called IM SAFE before every flight: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Eating. If any line fails, the flight doesn't happen. The captain who calls fatigue is the good captain. The system trusts the call.

Act IV — The framework

Read the Gauge before you fly

The instrument
The Gauge
your honest capacity reading — felt sense, post-sleep most reliable
Read it at every boundary:
post-sleep (mandatory) · after recovery · after a turn closes · between turns · mid-push if reserves drop

The Gauge is the answer to "how much have I got right now?" Its first job isn't to record what happened — it's to set the mode for what comes next. Read it before any phase fires. Re-read it after every recovery. The reading dispatches the next move.

A turn has six phases — three kinds

The turn isn't a script. It's a rule book. Six phases, three categories, with rules about when each runs.

Act IV — The framework

Required: Frame & Debrief — the bookends

Every turn has these two phases. Frame opens; Debrief closes. Both run always — even when everything in between gets skipped. Without Frame you drift. Without Debrief the turn doesn't compound into learning.

Required
Frame
5–15 minutes
Function Set scope. Pick the single decision this turn must produce. Name what's not in this turn. Quick, deliberate, written down.
Required
Debrief
10–20 minutes
Function Capture what happened. Note what worked and what didn't. Set up the next Gauge read. The cycle closes here, not when the work ships.

Conditional: the work, gated by reality

When the world doesn't supply the precondition, the phase collapses to seconds or skips entirely. The structure adapts to what's actually true today.

Insertable: Recover — anywhere, anytime

Insertable
Recover
minutes to hours
Pick a recovery mode Detach · psychological off-time, the strongest predictor of next-turn capacity
Relax · low-arousal, parasympathetic — slow walk, hot bath, music
Master · a hard new thing — counterintuitively restorative
Choose · autonomy mode — do whatever you actually want

Recover can be inserted between any two phases. Pick the mode deliberately — generic "rest" is usually scrolling, which doesn't restore. After every Recover, re-read the Gauge.

Sleep is a special, mandatory Recover. It happens roughly every twenty-four hours regardless of plan, and it triggers a forced post-sleep Gauge read — the morning-after check, IM-SAFE-equivalent.

Act IV — The framework

The rule book: how the phases compose

Canonical order
Frame → Comprehend → Sync → Push → Debrief → Recover. The default sequence when reserves are high and all preconditions are met.
Conditional skipping
Conditional phases collapse or skip when their preconditions aren't met. The structure isn't a script — it's a checklist.
Recover anywhere · re-read after
Recover can be inserted between any two phases. After it, the Gauge is re-read. Sleep is a special insertion.
Days are reporting periods. Turns are work units. A turn can span sleep — work that ended on Sync at 6pm continues with Push at 8am, if the morning Gauge passes.

Act IV — The framework

If you feel great after 90 minutes of intensity, that's the warning

In compensatory mode, feeling-good is the warning sign — not the green light.

When demand exceeds capacity, the body and brain compensate by spending reserves you can't feel spending. Dopamine masks the cost. By the time you notice depletion, you're already in deficit. The flow-state advice that says "ride it as long as it lasts" is correct for tasks that end on their own. It's wrong for AI-native work, where the task can run forever and the only stop is internal.

The tripwire. Hard time-box on Push (around 2.5 hours). Pre-committed when fresh, executed regardless of how good it feels mid-flow. Combat aviation calls this "bingo fuel" — the turnback point is decided when the head is clear, and it's executed without negotiation when the time comes.

Act IV — The framework

Two disciplines run across all of it

These don't belong to a phase. They live across the structure. Both deserve their own practice.

╲╱ Transition

Every phase boundary is taxed. Closing one phase cleanly and opening the next with intent is its own skill. Cognitive science calls this "attention residue" and it's measured. Practice surface: water, walk, posture change, a verbal cue.

┄┄ Trim

Selection discipline within each phase. Don't comprehend everything, don't sync on everything, don't decide everything, don't capture everything. Lighter once Frame is doing its job — but never disappears.

Act IV — The framework

Build Frame first

The framework adapts to whatever's true. So which phase deserves the most attention first? Frame. Six reasons converge on the same answer:

  1. Multiplicative downstream effect. Better Frame means less Comprehend, less Sync, less Push, less Debrief, less Recover. No other phase has reach into all five others.
  2. Cheapest to design. 5–15 minutes, templatable, repeatable. A one-page checklist.
  3. The literature agrees. Reducing demand before committing to it is consistently the highest-ROI cognitive intervention across forty years of research.
  4. Measure twice, cut once. Frame is the measure-twice phase.
  5. Hardest to recover from if missed. A bad Frame can't really be fixed mid-turn. It corrupts everything downstream.
  6. Where AI-native is uniquely new. Demand reduction onto a durable knowledge layer has no pre-AI analogue. The most learning is here.

Build the Frame template. Run it for a week. Iterate. Then layer on the rest.
